## What We Learned

### On why digitalization fails (first-person account)
- David arrived with the right tools and the right intentions — and failed
- The failure was NOT technical. It was cultural: he didn't understand the organization as a system
- 90 years of history created unwritten rules, relationships, and ways of working that no software could capture
- His lesson: "Just by writing it down and following rules, it wouldn't work"
- **Product implication:** The product must be about understanding the SYSTEM (culture, relationships, unwritten rules), not just the processes. Process documentation is necessary but not sufficient.

### On the "campfire" method (PRODUCT-DEFINING INSIGHT)
- People hate documenting processes. They love telling stories.
- Stories and anecdotes contain tacit knowledge in a way that structured documentation doesn't
- "Do you remember when we served this client in that kind of way?" — these narratives capture HOW things are done
- Nostalgic storytelling bypasses tech fatigue and defensiveness
- The exceptions and special cases — the things that matter most — only come out in stories, not in process mapping
- David himself tested this: invited employees to a bookshop to share stories about company values. They told him 40-year-old stories about his great-grandmother that revealed the soul of the company.
- **Product implication:** Frame the extraction process as storytelling, not documentation. "Tell me about a time when..." not "Describe your process for..." The output is the same (captured knowledge), but the experience is completely different.

### On tech fatigue
- "Even for me as a techie, I have tech fatigue" — if the tech person is exhausted by tech talk, the 65-year-old founder is already checked out
- SMEs are bombarded with "AI this, digitalize that" messaging and are increasingly resistant
- "Tech should not feel like tech"
- **Product implication:** Never sell this as an AI product or a digitalization tool. Sell it as a knowledge-preservation service, a business-heritage project, a transition-support engagement. The AI is internal tooling, invisible to the customer.

### On direct product validation
- "To have more context would have made a lot of things easier" — direct YES from someone who lived the transition
- The product wouldn't replace experience (he's honest about that) but would:
  - Prevent or resolve conflicts faster (because you'd understand WHY people resist)
  - Make change management easier (because you'd know the 20-year-old history behind today's resistance)
  - Help the successor understand the organization as a system, not just a set of processes
- "It really comes down to how good you could capture it. The premise is you can do it, then it would be very helpful."

### On organizations as systems
- David thinks in terms of systemic organization design — the organization as a system that can't be changed from outside, only given impulses
- Every employee, every tool, all the history — all parts of the system, all interacting
- Understanding system dynamics makes conflicts solvable and change manageable
- Most change resistance traces back 10-20 years to events the current leadership doesn't even know about
- **Product implication:** The knowledge base we build isn't just operational documentation — it's a MAP OF THE SYSTEM. Who relates to whom, what happened when, why people believe what they believe. That's the context that makes everything else work.

### On the M&A value angle
- David engaged immediately when Wolf mentioned M&A: "Then it's very obvious — greater transparency increases valuation"
- He also suggested: you could argue 10% savings on ERP implementation costs through better preparation
- The M&A framing gives a concrete, immediate ROI that traditional CEOs can grasp

### New

**Pattern 28 — The campfire method: stories capture what processes can't**
People hate documenting processes but love telling stories. Anecdotes and self-narratives capture tacit knowledge — the "how we do things" and "what makes us us" — more truthfully than structured process mapping. The exceptions, the judgment calls, the client-handling approaches all emerge through storytelling, not through questionnaires. Product implication: frame extraction as nostalgic storytelling, not documentation. "Tell me about a time when..." instead of "Describe your process for..."
- **Source:** [[Interview 013 — David (Textile Manufacturing, Germany)]]
- **Strength:** Strong — tested by David himself (bookshop storytelling sessions with employees) with powerful results

**Pattern 29 — Tech must not feel like tech**
SMEs have tech fatigue. Even tech-native successors have tech fatigue. "AI," "digitalization," "automation" are trigger words that provoke resistance, not interest. The product must be invisible as technology. Sell it as knowledge preservation, business heritage, transition support — never as an AI tool or a tech product. The AI is internal tooling; the customer experience is human conversation.
- **Source:** [[Interview 013 — David (Textile Manufacturing, Germany)]]
- **Strength:** Strong — from a former fintech engineer who experienced tech fatigue firsthand in his own company

**Pattern 30 — The 20-year ghost: resistance traces to forgotten history**
Most change resistance in SMEs traces back to events 10-20 years in the past that the current leadership doesn't even know about. Understanding this hidden history — the "system dynamics" — makes conflicts solvable and change manageable. The knowledge base isn't just operational: it's a map of the organization's emotional and relational history.
- **Source:** [[Interview 013 — David (Textile Manufacturing, Germany)]]
- **Strength:** Moderate — theoretical framing from one source, but resonates with [[Interview 011 — Dairy Chemicals Commercial (Italy)]] (relational knowledge as non-transferable)
